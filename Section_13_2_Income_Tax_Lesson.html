Personal Income Tax

Section 13.2  |  Business Mathematics  |  2022 tax tables

The big idea

Every year, people who earn money pay federal income tax. The tax is not figured on everything you earn. You start with your total income, take away certain amounts, and then use a tax rate schedule to find the tax on what is left.

Here is the path every problem in this section follows:

Total income− adjustments = Adjusted gross income− deduction = Taxable income→ schedule → Tax owed

At the end, you compare the tax owed with the tax that was already withheld from your paychecks to see if you get a refund or owe more.

1. Income and adjusted gross income (AGI)

  • Income from jobs (wages or salary) is the money you earn from working.
  • Interest is money a bank or credit union pays you on savings.
  • Dividend income is money a company pays you because you own its stock.
  • Miscellaneous (other) income is anything else, such as tips, prizes, or side jobs.
  • Adjustments to income are special amounts the law lets you subtract before anything else, such as IRA contributions, student loan interest, or educator expenses.
  • Adjusted gross income (AGI) is your total income minus your adjustments.
AGI = (Jobs + Interest + Dividends + Miscellaneous income) − Adjustments to income
Example (Worksheet #1)
Income from jobs $23,180, interest $335, miscellaneous income $2,494, dividends $55, and adjustments $2,878.
Total income: $23,180 + $335 + $2,494 + $55 = $26,064.
AGI: $26,064 − $2,878 = $23,186.

2. Filing status

Your filing status tells which standard deduction and which tax schedule to use. Problems show it with a letter after the name.

  • S = Single. Not married. Uses Schedule X.
  • M = Married filing jointly. A married couple files one return together. Uses Schedule Y-1.
  • MS = Married filing separately. Each spouse files his or her own return. Uses Schedule Y-2.
  • HH = Head of household. Not married and pays more than half the cost of a home for a dependent, such as a child. Uses Schedule Z.

3. Deductions: standard or itemized

A deduction is an amount you subtract from your AGI. You get to choose one of two kinds, and you always choose the larger one because it lowers your tax more.

The standard deduction is a set amount that depends only on your filing status:

  • Single: $12,950
  • Married filing jointly: $25,900
  • Married filing separately: $12,950
  • Head of household: $19,400

Itemized deductions are your actual expenses that the law allows you to list, and add up. The common ones are:

  • State and local taxes: state income tax, city income tax, and property (real estate) tax.
  • Mortgage interest: the interest paid on a home loan.
  • Charitable contributions: gifts to churches and charities.
Limit: state and local taxes together can count for at most $10,000. For married filing separately, the limit is $5,000.
Example (Worksheet #15)
Liz Karsten is married filing separately. She paid $14,300 in state income tax and $8,900 in real estate tax. That is $23,200 of state and local taxes, but she can count only $5,000.
Her itemized deductions are $7,630 mortgage interest + $5,000 taxes + $13,000 contributions = $25,630.

4. Taxable income

Taxable income = AGI − the larger deduction
Example (Worksheet #5)
R. Rodriguez is single with an AGI of $36,460 and itemized deductions of $4,900.
The standard deduction for single is $12,950. Since $12,950 is larger than $4,900, use the standard deduction.
Taxable income: $36,460 − $12,950 = $23,510.

5. Using a tax rate schedule

Each row of a schedule gives a range of taxable income. You find the row where your income fits, then use that row's rule:

Tax = Base amount + Rate × (Taxable income − "amount over" number)

The rate is used only on the part of your income above the start of the row. The base amount already covers the tax on everything below it. This is why moving into a higher bracket never makes your whole income taxed at the higher rate.

Example (continuing Rodriguez, Schedule X)
$23,510 is over $10,275 but not over $41,775. That row says $1,027.50 + 12% of the amount over $10,275.
Amount over: $23,510 − $10,275 = $13,235.
12% of that: 0.12 × $13,235 = $1,588.20.
Add the base: $1,027.50 + $1,588.20 = $2,615.70.

All four schedules are on the Tax Tables tab, and each practice problem shows the table it needs.

6. Withholding: refund or tax due

Employers take federal income tax out of each paycheck. This is called withholding. To find the total for the year, multiply by the number of paychecks:

  • Weekly: multiply by 52.
  • Monthly: multiply by 12.
  • If more was withheld than the tax owed, you get a refund of the difference.
  • If less was withheld than the tax owed, you owe the difference (tax due).
Example (Worksheet #8)
L. Karecki is single with a taxable income of $30,650, and $74.46 was withheld weekly.
Tax: $1,027.50 + 0.12 × ($30,650 − $10,275) = $1,027.50 + $2,445.00 = $3,472.50.
Withheld: $74.46 × 52 = $3,871.92.
More was withheld than owed, so Karecki gets a refund of $399.42 ($3,871.92 − $3,472.50).

7. Putting it all together

Example (Worksheet #11)
The Tobins are married filing jointly with an AGI of $99,500. Their deductions are $2,790 state income tax, $492 city income tax, $4,500 property tax, $5,700 mortgage interest, and $1,350 contributions.
Itemized total: $2,790 + $492 + $4,500 + $5,700 + $1,350 = $14,832.
Standard deduction for married filing jointly is $25,900, which is larger. Use the standard deduction.
Taxable income: $99,500 − $25,900 = $73,600.
Schedule Y-1: $73,600 is over $20,550 but not over $83,550, so the tax is $2,055 + 0.12 × ($73,600 − $20,550) = $2,055 + $6,366 = $8,421.00.
